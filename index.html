New here? How to read this page — discount, σ and buy/sell zones3-minute read

1 · What is NAV?

An investment company (like Investor or Latour) is basically a basket of other companies. NAV (net asset value, substansvärde) is what that basket is worth per share: the value of everything it owns, minus its debt.

2 · Discount and premium

The share price on the stock market is often not the same as NAV.

Discount: price is below NAV. A 10% discount means you pay 90 kr for 100 kr worth of assets.
Premium: price is above NAV. A 10% premium means you pay 110 kr for 100 kr worth of assets.

3 · Every company has its own "normal"

Some companies almost always trade at a premium (investors pay extra for a strong track record or skilled owners), others almost always at a discount (unlisted holdings, costs, a less popular strategy). So a 5% premium can be expensive for one company and cheap for another. That is why this page compares each company with its own history rather than with zero.

4 · Average and σ (sigma)

We look at the last 7 years of daily values (or all data, if a company is younger) and calculate two things:

Normal level: the 7-year average discount or premium. A long period covers both good and bad markets.
σ, standard deviation: how much it normally swings around that average over the same 7 years. Small σ = calm and stable; large σ = it jumps around a lot.

As a rule of thumb, values stay within ±1σ of the average about two-thirds of the time, and beyond ±2σ only about 1 time in 20.

5 · The z-score

The z-score answers one question: how many "normal swings" away from its usual level is the company today?

z = (discount today − normal level) ÷ σ

z = 0 means exactly normal. z = +2 means the discount is two normal swings wider than usual (cheaper). z = −2 means two swings narrower than usual, or a premium (more expensive).

6 · Buy zone and sell zone

Buy zone (z ≥ +1): the discount is unusually wide, so the share is unusually cheap compared with its own history.

Sell zone (z ≤ −1): the discount is unusually narrow or there is an unusual premium, so the share is unusually expensive compared with its own history.

Between −1 and +1 is neutral: nothing unusual.

WHY IT CAN WORK
Discounts and premiums tend to drift back towards their normal level over time. In the backtest below, buying in the buy zone beat the average 12-month return for most companies.
WHY IT CAN FAIL
"Normal" can change for good: Investor and Latour have moved from discount to premium over the years. A company can also be cheap for a reason, such as falling asset values or a weak strategy.
HOW TO USE IT
Treat the zones as a starting point for your own research, not as a buy or sell button. Look at the holdings, the long-term chart and the 5/7-year average before deciding anything.

Have the signals worked?

Each trading day the company is placed in a zone using only the data available that day (its previous 7 years, or all earlier data if less; the test starts once a company has 5 years of history), then the share price change over the next 12 months (250 trading days) is measured. Price return excludes dividends but is adjusted for share splits and spin-offs. Discount change is in percentage points; negative means the discount narrowed.

How to read this table
  • 12m return after buy zone: imagine buying the share on every day the company was in the buy zone and selling exactly 12 months later. This is the average share price change of all those trades. Green = better than buying on any random day (the "all days" column).
  • Share up: how many of those trades ended with a higher share price after 12 months. 75% means three out of four.
  • 12m return after sell zone: the same, but for days in the sell zone. Red = worse than a random day, which is what you would expect if the sell signal works.
  • 12m return all days: the benchmark, buying on any day regardless of zone.
  • Discount change: how much the discount moved during those 12 months. In the buy zone you hope for a negative number (the unusually wide discount shrinks back towards normal); in the sell zone a positive one. The discount can return to normal while the share price still falls, if the whole market and the company's holdings fall at the same time.
  • Days buy / sell: how many trading days the signal was on. Few days (for example under 50) usually means a single short episode, so treat that result with extra caution.

Method and caveats
Source
The price history CSV that ibindex.se offers for each company: share price, reported NAV and ibindex's calculated NAV per trading day. All investment companies ibindex tracks are included: the companies in the ibindex index, plus the others it lists (marked °). Companies with under 2 years of data are shown but not given a zone. A GitHub Action downloads the files every Friday and recomputes everything.
Discount
(NAV − price) / NAV. Positive is a discount, negative a premium. The daily calculated NAV is used where available (from November 2016, Linc from May 2021); before that the latest reported NAV.
z-score and zones
(today − 7-year average) / 7-year standard deviation, over about 1,750 trading days (all data if a company has less). Zones and tables use daily values. The average line in the charts is the simple average of month-end values over the last 5 or 7 years (or since the data starts, if shorter).
Holdings
The five largest listed holdings as a share of NAV, plus unlisted holdings as one total and cash or interest-bearing assets where significant, from ibindex's latest reported holdings (the date is shown with each company). Where ibindex files listed remainders or interest-bearing assets under unlisted, they are counted as listed and cash instead.
Share splits and spin-offs
ibindex's price history is not adjusted for share splits (for example Investor's 4:1 split in 2021) or distributions of shares to owners. When the price and NAV jump by about the same large ratio on one day, the backtest treats that day as a corporate action rather than a gain or loss.
Caveats
The 12-month windows overlap, so days within a zone are not independent observations and each company only has a handful of separate episodes. Returns exclude dividends. A discount can persist for a long time, and a company's normal level can shift permanently. Past patterns say nothing certain about the future.
